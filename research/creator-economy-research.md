# Creator-economy research: findings, patterns and design implications

**Scope:** how Nigerian creators build sustainable businesses from content, expertise and community. The research supports the design of *Creator Hustle* and is not an academic study.
**Method:** web search and direct reading of creator interviews, case studies, journalism and report announcements (accessed 13 October 2026). Structured records: [`creator-stories.json`](creator-stories.json). Sources: [`sources.md`](sources.md). Mechanics mapping: [`design-mapping.md`](design-mapping.md). Caveats: [`research-limitations.md`](research-limitations.md).

## 1. What was collected

- **24 structured records**: 21 individuals or small groups with publicly documented stories, 2 organisations (OkadaBooks, Calabar Tech Community) and 1 non-Nigerian comparator (Ransford, Ghana/Canada), clearly flagged.
- **Categories covered:** digital-product educators (WakaWaka Doctor, Social Media Oga, Felix Ohaeri, Passive Income Anna, Chidera Peters); writers (Busayo Joan, Stephanie Odili, OkadaBooks' founder); video/YouTube creators (MissTechy, Eric Okafor, Ajebo, Chibuzor Ossai, Polan, Fareedah Abdulsalaam); services/freelance (Busayo Joan, Chibuzor Ossai, Olamide Femi-Taiwo, Your Location Girl); community and events (Calabar Tech Community, Oo Nwoye, Social Media Oga's community); creators who started while employed or as students (Chidera Peters, Fareedah Abdulsalaam, Olamide Femi-Taiwo, Social Media Oga); and setback or failure stories (Pasheda, Passive Income Anna, Felix Ohaeri's earlier ventures, OkadaBooks' closure, Oo Nwoye's Callbase).
- **Geography:** only recorded where the source states it. Verified mentions include Lagos (Busayo Joan, Polan), Delta State (Chibuzor Ossai's origin), Kaduna State (Chidera Peters' workshops) and Calabar, Cross River State. Most sources do not state a region, and none were invented.
- **Gaps:** few strong first-person sources for podcasters and independent musicians, because the relevant pages returned errors or were paywalled. See the limitations document.

## 2. Recurring patterns

Each pattern has an ID used in the JSON records and the design mapping.

| ID | Pattern | Evidence (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | **Start small, with what you have.** First products are cheap and quick; first equipment is borrowed or basic. | Polan filmed on a borrowed phone; Passive Income Anna made an ebook in Canva in under two hours; Your Location Girl started on an old phone with no lighting. |
| P2 | **Expertise and audience questions become products.** | WakaWaka Doctor turned visa questions into an ebook and consultations; Chidera Peters packaged career and budgeting knowledge; Olamide Femi-Taiwo moved from design services to a course. |
| P3 | **Relevance beats raw reach.** Engaged, niche audiences convert; big views can pay little. | BusinessDay webinar: fame isn't required. Ajebo had 13M+ views but earnings below expectations. Your Location Girl stresses engaged followers. Condia notes that niche fit matters more than follower count. |
| P4 | **Affiliates extend distribution, at the cost of margin.** | WakaWaka Doctor used affiliates; Passive Income Anna promotes other creators' products; Success Mba funded a course with affiliate income. |
| P5 | **Pricing is hard; underpricing is common.** | Chidera Peters feared underpricing; Your Location Girl refused a low fee and set a rate card; Social Media Oga started with a ₦3,000 class. |
| P6 | **Diversify from one topic into related offers.** | WakaWaka Doctor: ebook, consulting, membership. Social Media Oga: free PDF, class, toolkit, community. Chibuzor Ossai: brand work, then classes. |
| P7 | **Paid ads can scale a proven offer when return is tracked.** | Felix Ohaeri reports spending about a third of earnings on ads for "about three times return" (self-reported outlier). |
| P8 | **Weak launches and failures are common, and recoverable.** | Passive Income Anna: six copies on day one, later growth after more promotion; Pasheda: failed business, then a recovered brand; Felix Ohaeri: earlier failed ventures. |
| P9 | **Platform risk.** Accounts get suspended or restricted. | Passive Income Anna's repeated Instagram suspensions; Social Media Oga lost a 35k-follower account; Condia cites a TikTok livestream restriction. |
| P10 | **Customer care builds trust.** | Pasheda replaced a bad batch generously and gained a loyal referrer. |
| P11 | **Time and energy limit growth; burnout is real.** | Selar burnout roundup; Social Media Oga's constant work; Fareedah balancing a full-time job; Ransford's strain from 1:1 sessions (comparator). |
| P12 | **Infrastructure and payment friction.** | NECLive/Frontyard 2026: infrastructure, administrative burden and payment-rail friction are the main constraints. OkadaBooks' founder found collecting payment harder than selling. Techpoint 2019: data costs and connectivity shape viewing. Social Media Oga wants dollar income. |
| P13 | **Communities compound and can become events.** | Calabar Tech Community grew from 5 people in a WhatsApp group to a ~750-attendee conference; Oo Nwoye's meetups became an annual ecosystem party. |
| P14 | **Piracy and content theft.** | Selar plagiarism article (view-only hosting, watermarking); WakaWaka Doctor's read-online delivery; Ajebo's re-uploaded videos. |
| P15 | **Discoverability is a writer's bottleneck.** | Stephanie Odili's distribution challenge; OkadaBooks' story. |
| P16 | **Brand deals are a common but uneven income stream.** | Techpoint 2019: brand integrations outweigh AdSense for most; Polan, Fareedah and Chibuzor Ossai describe brand work; Condia notes late payments and missing contracts. |

## 3. Answers to the research questions

**How do creators convert attention into purchases?** Through a warm, relevant audience and a frictionless checkout: a single store link (WakaWaka Doctor), launch teasers to an engaged audience (Social Media Oga), webinars and practical classes (Passive Income Anna), and free lead magnets that build a direct list (Social Media Oga's PDF, Chidera Peters' free budgeting tools). *Game: conversion depends on audience fit, channel trust, price, quality and reputation, not follower count alone.*

**How does expertise become digital products?** Professionals package what they repeatedly explain: visas, CVs, budgeting, editing, social media. *Game: paths start with skill advantages in certain product types; niche-topic products convert best.*

**What role do affiliates, partnerships and direct relationships play?** Affiliates extend reach beyond a creator's own audience. Email and communities protect against platform risk. Collaborations bring relevant followers. *Game: four channels with distinct cost, reach, fit and trust; affiliate commissions reduce margin; email has the highest trust.*

**Why diversify?** To serve different willingness to pay (free → cheap → premium → recurring) and reuse one topic. *Game: "proven topic" bonus makes related products cheaper, faster and more in demand.*

**How do production capacity and fulfilment limit growth?** Coaching and communities consume hours; burnout lowers quality. *Game: daily hour budget, coaching slots, membership upkeep, event hosting, wellbeing.*

**What are the trade-offs between audience, revenue and profit?** Big views can pay little; ads can buy revenue at a loss; tracked expenses are rare (Social Media Oga). *Game: separate revenue, gross profit and net profit; the win condition requires profit and satisfaction, not revenue alone; the score weights profit above revenue.*

**How do infrastructure and payment friction affect operations?** Power, data, payment rails and delayed brand payments create cash-flow and time shocks. *Game: power-cut, network, payment-delay and repair events, each with backup-upgrade counterplay; revenue settles into cash a day later.*

**How can creators recover from a weak launch?** Improve the product, keep promoting, change price, add channels, and look after customers. *Game: Improve action, price changes, featured product, generous-refund choices, side gigs as a cash lifeline.*

## 4. Industry context (secondary, used qualitatively)

- *The State of Nigeria's Creative Economy 2026* (NECLive/Frontyard; 377 respondents across eight sectors): the main constraints are ecosystem and infrastructure (infrastructure deficits, administrative burden, payment-rail friction, financing and training), not talent. Only the announcement was read.
- *Nigeria Creator Economy Report 2025*, as covered by TechCabal through search summaries only: a majority of creators reportedly earn under $100 a month and a small minority earn over $5,000. The primary report was not read; treat as indicative.
- Selar reported ₦9.88bn paid to creators in 2024 (platform-published).
- Spotify reported ₦58bn in royalties to Nigerian artists in 2024 (Condia, citing Spotify Loud & Clear). Streaming alone is described as insufficient for most artists.

These figures were **not** used as game balance values. The game uses its own configurable values (see `src/config/balance.ts`).

## 5. Design implications kept, and findings deliberately left out

**Kept:** relevance-weighted conversion, lead magnets and email, affiliates with commission, paid ads with diminishing returns and fatigue, proven-topic diversification, time/energy capacity, infrastructure events with backup upgrades, payout delays, piracy, platform restrictions, brand deals, recoverable failures, and the requirement to be profitable.

**Left out (not fun, not fair, or not well-evidenced):** multi-currency and tax mechanics (too fiddly for a 15-minute game), family financial obligations (sensitive and better left to players' own interpretation), forex/trading products (financial-risk concerns), and specific earnings benchmarks (single success stories are not representative).
